Reckoning & Remembrance: the Louisiana Native Guards History Mural

In September 1862, free men of color answered a call to join the Union Army at 3300 Royal Street. More than 160 years later, we are painting their story across the very wall where they mustered — a permanent, 2,000-square-foot commemorative mural in the Bywater.

The History

The Civil War at Beanlandia’s Site

Our building at 3300 Royal Street was once the Touro Almshouse — the first headquarters of the Louisiana Native Guards, the first Black Union soldiers officially mustered into the United States Army.

In September 1862, Union commander General Benjamin Butler called for free men of color to join the Army. The response was immediate and overwhelming. Those men — later the Corps d’Afrique — went on to fight at the Battle of Port Hudson in May 1863, the first major assault led by Black soldiers in American history.

Much of this history has been buried or rewritten. The mural is our way of putting it back in plain sight, on the wall where it started.

Some of the men who were at Beanlandia

  • •Cpt. André Cailloux — a free man of color and celebrated officer of the Native Guards, killed heroically at Port Hudson. His funeral galvanized Black New Orleans. He called himself “the Blackest man in New Orleans.”
  • •Gen. Benjamin Butler — the Union commander who authorized the recruitment of free Black men into the U.S. Army, a radical act that helped change the course of the Civil War.
  • •P.B.S. Pinchback — Native Guards officer, a leading recruiter of Black soldiers in New Orleans, and later the first Black governor of Louisiana.
  • •Charles Sauvinet — New Orleans’ first Black civil sheriff, a Native Guards officer, and a civil rights pioneer who successfully challenged segregation in Louisiana courts.
  • •The soldiers — the hundreds of free men of color and formerly enslaved men who answered the call, including two men from the Congo. Their names and faces deserve to be seen.

Painted by New Orleans artists Annie Moran and Tenielle Prosper. Led by the Krewe of Red Beans and the André Cailloux Center for Performing Arts and Cultural Justice, in partnership with Arts New Orleans. You can read the full artist call (PDF).

Be Part of It

Give $100 or more, and your name goes up beside the mural

This mural will stand on Royal Street for decades. Donors who give $100 or more will have their name listed on the donor sign installed next to it — a permanent record of the people who helped bring this history back into the light.

How it works

  1. Give $100 or more using the form below. Pick $100, $250, $500, $1,000 — or enter any amount you like.
  2. We use the name on your donation. Want it spelled differently, listed as a family, or given in memory of someone? Email beans@kreweofredbeans.org after you give and we’ll take care of it.
  3. Your name goes on the sign. The donor sign is installed alongside the finished mural at 3300 Royal Street.

Giving less than $100? Please do — every dollar goes to the mural, and you’ll still be part of making it happen. Prefer to stay anonymous at any amount? Just let us know.

The Krewe of Red Beans is a 501(c)(3) nonprofit — your gift is tax-deductible. See exactly where the money goes on our transparency page.

Come see the wall

The mural site is our own building at 3300 Royal Street — home of the Bean Museum. Stop by Monday through Friday, 10am–5pm, and see where the Native Guards began.
